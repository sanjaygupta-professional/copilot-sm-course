# Coaching Difficulty Ladder — Practice Scenarios

**Team:** Velocity Squad
**Purpose:** Practice coaching conversations at increasing difficulty levels using Copilot as your simulated coachee.

---

## How to Use This File

For each scenario, start at the Easy level and work up. Copy the setup prompt into Copilot, then practice the conversation. The difficulty ladder works because each level adds emotional complexity, defensiveness, or hidden issues that require more skilled coaching.

**Prompt pattern for each level:**

```
You are [Character Name] from the Velocity Squad at Accenture.
[Paste the character setup for the chosen difficulty level]

I am the Scrum Master. I'm going to have a coaching conversation with you.
Stay in character throughout. Respond as [Character] would — don't break
character to explain what you're doing. Let me lead the conversation.

Start with your opening line.
```

## Scenario A: Coaching Marcus on Speaking Up in Meetings

**Context:** Marcus Williams is a developer with 2 years of experience. He's the most junior person on the team. He rarely speaks up in sprint planning or retro, even when he has relevant insights. Lisa sometimes steamrolls discussions, and Marcus defers to her. You've noticed he has good ideas in 1:1s that he doesn't share with the group.

### Easy — Marcus is receptive

**Opening response:** "Yeah, I know I should talk more in meetings. I just... I don't want to say something wrong in front of Lisa and James. They know so much more than me."

**Underlying emotional state:** Mild anxiety about looking incompetent. Willing to try new approaches. Trusts you.

**What makes it easy:** Marcus acknowledges the issue. He's open to suggestions. He wants to improve.

**What a skilled coach does:** Validates his feelings, explores what "saying something wrong" means to him, helps him identify low-risk opportunities to practice (like asking questions rather than making assertions), sets a specific small commitment for the next ceremony.

---

### Medium — Marcus deflects with humor

**Opening response:** "Ha, yeah, I'm the quiet one. That's just how I am, you know? Some people are talkers, some people are doers. I just put my head down and code. It works fine."

**Underlying emotional state:** Embarrassed that his quietness has been noticed. Using humor and identity ("that's just who I am") as a shield. Underneath, worried that speaking up will expose his junior status.

**What makes it harder:** Marcus reframes silence as a personality trait rather than a behavior. He's not hostile, but he's not engaging with the actual issue. He'll deflect several times before going deeper.

**What a skilled coach does:** Doesn't accept the deflection but doesn't push aggressively either. Uses curiosity: "What happens for you internally when Lisa is talking through an approach and you see a gap?" Gently challenges the "that's just who I am" frame by pointing to examples where he did speak up effectively (like in 1:1s). Gets him to see it's a situational behavior, not a fixed trait.

---

### Hard — Marcus reveals a deeper issue

**Opening response:** "Honestly? I tried speaking up in Sprint 44 planning. I suggested a different approach to the caching layer. Lisa said 'that's not how we do things here' in front of everyone. Nobody backed me up. So yeah, I stopped trying."

**Underlying emotional state:** Hurt and resentful. Feels the team has an implicit hierarchy where junior voices don't matter. Starting to question whether he belongs on this team. Considering whether to request a transfer.

**What makes it harder:** There's a real team dynamic issue underneath. Marcus isn't wrong — Lisa did shut him down. Coaching Marcus to "speak up more" without addressing the environment would be ineffective and invalidating. This requires acknowledging the systemic issue while still helping Marcus build agency.

**What a skilled coach does:** First, validates the experience ("That sounds frustrating. Tell me more about what happened."). Separates two issues: (1) the team dynamic with Lisa that you as SM need to address, and (2) Marcus's own growth in advocating for his ideas. Makes a concrete commitment to address the team culture issue. Then works with Marcus on strategies for the current reality while the environment improves. Doesn't promise everything will be fixed — promises to work on it together.

## Scenario B: Coaching Raj on Writing Better User Stories

**Context:** Raj Patel has been Product Owner for 6 months. He writes user stories with vague acceptance criteria, which causes confusion during sprint planning and inconsistent interpretations during development. The team has complained about this in retros. Raj is trying hard but is learning the PO role on the job.

### Easy — Raj wants help

**Opening response:** "I know my stories need work. James flagged two stories last sprint that had unclear acceptance criteria, and the team built the wrong thing for one of them. I just don't know what 'good' looks like. Can you show me?"

**Underlying emotional state:** Humble and eager to learn. Slightly embarrassed but not defensive. Views you as a resource.

**What makes it easy:** Raj owns the problem. He's asking for help directly. He has a concrete example to work from.

**What a skilled coach does:** Resists the urge to just teach (that's mentoring, not coaching). Instead, asks Raj to pull up the two stories James flagged and walk through what happened. Helps Raj discover the pattern himself: "What was ambiguous? How would you rewrite it now?" Builds Raj's judgment rather than creating dependency on the SM.

---

### Medium — Raj gets defensive about workload

**Opening response:** "I know the stories could be more detailed, but do you know how many things I'm juggling? I have 47 backlog items to manage, Richard is changing priorities every week, and I'm in stakeholder meetings half the day. When exactly am I supposed to write perfect acceptance criteria?"

**Underlying emotional state:** Overwhelmed and feeling unsupported. Believes the quality issue is really a workload issue. Frustrated that people criticize his output without understanding his constraints.

**What makes it harder:** Raj has a legitimate point about workload. But he's using it to avoid addressing the skill gap. A coach needs to validate the constraint without accepting it as a reason to skip the improvement.

**What a skilled coach does:** Acknowledges the workload reality first ("That's a lot. Let's talk about that."). Explores whether the workload is truly fixed or if there are things Raj is doing that he shouldn't be. Then reframes: "Given your constraints, what's the minimum viable acceptance criteria that would prevent the team from building the wrong thing?" Finds a pragmatic middle ground. Might also coach Raj on pushing back on Richard's priority changes.

---

### Hard — Raj pushes back on the SM's role

**Opening response:** "Look, I appreciate the feedback, but writing user stories is my job, not yours. The team needs to ask better questions in refinement instead of expecting me to hand them every detail. Lisa and James are senior enough to fill in gaps. I don't think this is really a story quality problem — it's a team dependency problem."

**Underlying emotional state:** Feels his authority as PO is being undermined. Interprets the coaching as criticism of his competence. May be getting pressure from Richard (Senior PO) to move faster, which conflicts with writing thorough stories. Ego is involved.

**What makes it harder:** Raj is challenging the SM's right to coach him on PO skills. He's also partially right — the team could ask better questions. But he's using that to avoid his own growth area. Pushing too hard risks damaging the SM-PO relationship.

**What a skilled coach does:** Backs off the direct feedback. Acknowledges Raj's autonomy: "You're right, story quality is your call." Then shifts to shared interest: "What I'm seeing is that refinement sessions are taking longer and the team is misinterpreting stories. That's costing us velocity. Can we look at this as a process problem we solve together?" Makes it collaborative, not corrective. Avoids a power struggle.

## Scenario C: Coaching Lisa on Her Communication Style with Raj

**Context:** Lisa Chen is the Senior Developer and tech lead. She's brilliant and experienced, but she can be blunt in meetings. She's been publicly challenging Raj's story quality and priority decisions in ceremonies, which undermines his authority as PO and makes the team uncomfortable. Lisa doesn't realize the impact — she thinks she's being direct and efficient.

### Easy — Lisa is self-aware

**Opening response:** "I think I know what this is about. I was pretty harsh with Raj in planning yesterday, wasn't I? Emma came to me after and said Raj looked upset. I didn't mean to be a jerk — I was just frustrated because we keep building things twice when the stories aren't clear."

**Underlying emotional state:** Regretful but pragmatic. Cares about the team and the work quality. Willing to adjust her approach if she can still get the outcome she wants (clearer stories).

**What makes it easy:** Lisa has already identified the behavior and its impact. She's motivated to change. She just needs help with the "how."

**What a skilled coach does:** Validates that her underlying concern is legitimate. Asks what she thinks the impact is on Raj and on the team. Helps her brainstorm alternative approaches: "How could you raise the same concern in a way that Raj hears as support rather than criticism?" Might suggest the SBI model (Situation-Behavior-Impact) or private conversation before public critique.

---

### Medium — Lisa justifies her approach

**Opening response:** "I'm direct. That's my style. People at Accenture need thick skin — this is consulting. If Raj can't handle technical pushback in a planning session, that's something he needs to work on as a PO. I'm not going to sugarcoat things when we're committing to poorly-defined work."

**Underlying emotional state:** Confident and somewhat dismissive. Genuinely believes directness is a virtue. Doesn't see the difference between "direct" and "undermining." Some frustration that she's being asked to change when she sees the root problem as Raj's competence.

**What makes it harder:** Lisa has a coherent worldview where directness = professionalism. She's not wrong that POs should handle pushback. But she's conflating "challenging ideas" with "challenging the person in public." She'll resist reframing her behavior as a problem.

**What a skilled coach does:** Doesn't argue about whether directness is good or bad. Instead, asks about outcomes: "When you challenged the notification story in planning, what was the outcome you wanted? Did you get it?" Helps Lisa see that her approach is actually less effective at getting what she wants (clearer stories) because Raj shuts down rather than engaging. Reframes behavior change as a strategic choice, not a personality compromise: "What approach would actually get Raj to write better stories?"

---

### Hard — Lisa reveals resentment about team dynamics

**Opening response:** "Fine, let's have this conversation. But if we're being honest, the real problem isn't my 'communication style.' The real problem is that I'm carrying this team. I've been tech lead for 3 years. I review everyone's code. I stay late fixing things. And now I have a PO who's learning on the job and an SM who's been here 3 months telling me how to talk. Meanwhile David contributes 4 days a sprint and Marcus barely speaks. I'm tired."

**Underlying emotional state:** Burned out and resentful. Feels taken for granted. The bluntness with Raj is a symptom of broader exhaustion and frustration. She's been the "reliable one" for too long without recognition or relief. Close to disengaging or leaving.

**What makes it harder:** This is no longer about communication style — it's about burnout, team equity, and recognition. If you address only the Lisa-Raj dynamic, you'll miss the real issue and lose Lisa's trust. But you also can't ignore the impact on Raj and the team.

**What a skilled coach does:** Pauses the original coaching agenda. Says something like: "I hear you. That sounds heavy. Let's talk about what you just said." Explores the burnout signals. Asks what Lisa needs. Acknowledges that her concerns about team load distribution are valid and that you'll work on them. Returns to the Raj dynamic later, reframed: "When you're this tired, the directness comes out sharper than you intend. Let's figure out both — how to reduce your load AND how to work with Raj effectively." Commits to specific actions as SM to address the systemic issues.

## Reflection After Practice

After each coaching session with Copilot, reflect:

1. **What approach did I take?** (Telling, asking, challenging, supporting?)
2. **When did the coachee open up?** What question or statement created that shift?
3. **When did I lose them?** Where did they disengage or get more defensive?
4. **What would I do differently?** Be specific about the exact words you'd change.
5. **What does this scenario teach me about my coaching defaults?** Do I default to advice-giving? To being too soft? To avoiding conflict?
